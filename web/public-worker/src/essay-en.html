Model

The key idea

Cash redundancy is capital that is not committed to a thesis: it exists so that a mistake, a margin call, or a market that stops trading does not force a sale. Leverage multiplies the outcome when the model is right and turns that same mistake into a liquidity obligation when the model is wrong.

Model

A practical analogy

In an electrical system sized to an N+1 criterion, the standby generator does not raise the output of the circuit already feeding the load. It takes space, needs maintenance, and sits idle most of the time. Its role appears when the primary line fails: without that slack, the shortfall spreads and the rest of the system shuts down in cascade. Uninvested cash occupies the same place on a balance sheet. It is not a source of return; it is the capacity to keep operating when the source the plan depends on stops delivering.

Case

Real case study

Long-Term Capital Management L.P., a fund founded in February 1994 by John Meriwether, sought temporary price differences between similar securities and financed them with debt. The Federal Reserve historical essay records that, at the end of 1997, the fund held about $30 of debt per $1 of capital, after returning capital to investors without shrinking the size of the positions. In August 1998 Russia devalued its currency and defaulted on its debt. The spreads the fund expected to close widened instead, and the fund lost 44 percent of its value in that month alone.

On 23 September 1998, fourteen banks and brokerages put in $3.625 billion in exchange for 90 percent ownership. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York convened the parties and oversaw the arrangement; it did not put in its own funds. The partners kept a 10 percent stake. Under that control, the fund sold most of what remained and, by the end of 1999, had repaid the last tranche of the consortium contribution, which the same source puts at $3.6 billion. The cash that would have allowed risk to be reduced without a forced sale was not inside the vehicle: continuity depended on outside capital. The facts in this section are taken from the Federal Reserve essay on the episode, dated 22 November 2013 (federalreservehistory.org).

Model

Operating heuristic

  1. If the thesis takes twice as long to play out, which cash obligation comes due first?
  2. If several positions once treated as independent move together, does the remaining cash cover the margins without a forced sale?
  3. Does balance-sheet survival depend on a third party putting in capital on a day when the market does not want to buy?
